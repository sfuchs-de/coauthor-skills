# Economic Writing Dictionary

This is the curated explanatory dictionary, not a list of automatically approved
publication words. Apply [Corpus-restricted writing](VOCABULARY_POLICY.md) and
check [term attestation](vocabulary/curated_dictionary_attestation.csv). Some entries
are useful private editing labels or necessary technical distinctions whose wording
does not occur in the corpus; they require substitution or a justified exception
in future research prose.

185 entries are recommended usages, not a claim that every phrase appears in every paper. Examples are original and hypothetical, not quotations or new research findings. Literal source matches are separately recorded in the database; frequency is not a writing prescription.

## Argument

### research question
A specified economic change and outcome to be explained.

**Use carefully:** Name population and horizon, not only topic.

**Example:** We ask how seasonal visitor demand changes residents' purchasing power.

### contribution
The particular advance the paper establishes.

**Use carefully:** Do not substitute an adjective for the advance.

**Example:** The method links purchases to residents rather than assigning destination sales to local households.

### mechanism
Choices or interactions connecting a cause to an outcome.

**Use carefully:** A pattern compatible with a mechanism does not uniquely identify it.

**Example:** Workers spend part of their additional earnings away from their workplace.

### object of interest
The quantity needed to answer the question.

**Use carefully:** Replace this phrase with the object's name once defined.

**Example:** The object of interest is the change in residents' money-metric welfare.

### obstacle
The missing information or ambiguity preventing a direct answer.

**Use carefully:** Identify the obstacle rather than declaring the problem complex.

**Example:** Transactions reveal expenditure but do not separately reveal prices and quantities.

### implication
A consequence of a specified argument or model.

**Use carefully:** State the premises and whether the claim is qualitative or quantitative.

**Example:** Under fixed labor, a price increase raises returns on initial output.

### intuition
The economic reason a relationship holds.

**Use carefully:** Do not call an assertion intuition without explaining choices or incentives.

**Example:** Higher wages attract commuters who otherwise prefer another workplace.

### interpretation
The economic meaning attached to an estimate or object.

**Use carefully:** Keep interpretation separate from statistical significance.

**Example:** The estimate measures a proportional response, not a change in euros.

### scope
The set of environments or populations covered by a claim.

**Use carefully:** Do not generalize beyond maintained conditions.

**Example:** The result applies to nonhousing purchases on the modeled support.

### benchmark
The declared comparison specification or economy.

**Use carefully:** A benchmark is not necessarily the uniquely defensible model.

**Example:** The benchmark holds workplace assignments fixed.

### baseline
The initial account, period, or maintained specification.

**Use carefully:** Specify which meaning is intended.

**Example:** Baseline expenditure shares are measured before the shock.

### comparison
Two explicitly defined objects evaluated together.

**Use carefully:** Align samples, units, weights, and horizons.

**Example:** We compare local and nonlinear responses to the same demand change.

### contrast
A difference that advances the argument.

**Use carefully:** Name both sides rather than writing in contrast without a referent.

**Example:** Destination sales rise, while nearby residents redirect purchases elsewhere.

### handoff
The unresolved object passed to the next part of the argument.

**Use carefully:** Use as a revision label, not routine economic jargon in the draft.

**Example:** Valuation requires income and prices; the next subsection recovers those changes.

### roadmap
A concise explanation of the paper's order.

**Use carefully:** Do not repeat it at every heading.

**Example:** Theory establishes the recovery equations, and the data section constructs their inputs.

## Valuation

### first-order
The linear local term in a change around a specified point.

**Use carefully:** Does not establish an exact finite response.

**Example:** The first-order cost uses the initial consumption basket.

### second-order
A local term quadratic in the size of a smooth change.

**Use carefully:** State smoothness and avoid implying it is numerically negligible for a large shock.

**Example:** Substitution adds a second-order contribution locally under smoothness.

### envelope theorem
A derivative of an optimized value under appropriate conditions.

**Use carefully:** State timing, feasible choices, and regularity.

**Example:** The envelope isolates the direct effect of a price change at the initial optimum.

### Shephard's lemma
The price derivative of expenditure gives compensated demand under regularity.

**Use carefully:** Do not conflate compensated demand with any observed quantity change.

**Example:** Initial compensated purchases value a small price increase.

### expenditure function
Minimum spending required for a specified utility at given prices.

**Use carefully:** Define prices, utility, and any other arguments.

**Example:** The expenditure function expresses the cost of maintaining initial utility.

### indirect utility
Utility attained after optimizing choices subject to the budget.

**Use carefully:** Distinguish its units from money-metric welfare.

**Example:** Indirect utility depends on income and the prices the resident faces.

### equivalent variation
An income change at reference prices equivalent to a welfare change.

**Use carefully:** Declare the reference prices and denominator.

**Example:** We express equivalent variation as a share of initial income.

### compensating variation
An income adjustment at changed prices restoring reference utility.

**Use carefully:** Do not interchange it with equivalent variation for finite changes.

**Example:** Compensating variation values the adjustment at post-shock prices.

### money metric
A monetary representation of welfare at specified reference prices.

**Use carefully:** The reference price vector matters.

**Example:** The money metric uses the initial price vector throughout the comparison.

### initial basket
The quantities purchased before the change.

**Use carefully:** Do not replace quantities with expenditure shares without the required conversion.

**Example:** The initial basket costs more when destination prices rise.

### Laspeyres index
A fixed initial-quantity price index.

**Use carefully:** Finite fixed-basket valuation need not equal the exact cost-of-living change.

**Example:** The Laspeyres comparison holds initial quantities constant.

### Paasche index
A price comparison using final quantities.

**Use carefully:** Its bounding interpretation requires the relevant preference and expenditure conditions.

**Example:** The Paasche comparison uses the final consumption basket.

### cost of living
Expenditure required to attain a defined standard of utility.

**Use carefully:** A price average is not automatically a cost-of-living index.

**Example:** The cost-of-living calculation allows the specified substitution behavior.

### price index
An aggregation of prices under a declared formula or demand system.

**Use carefully:** Define its normalization and economic interpretation.

**Example:** The origin-specific index weights destinations through the resident's basket.

### price share
A declared marginal mapping from sales or revenue changes into price changes.

**Use carefully:** Distinguish an estimated derivative from an observed price or average share.

**Example:** Estimated price shares translate recovered sales changes into local price changes.

### restricted profit
An optimized return holding some inputs fixed.

**Use carefully:** Name the fixed and flexible inputs and their prices.

**Example:** Restricted profit holds workplace labor fixed while materials adjust.

### factor payments
Payments to labor, capital, or other productive inputs.

**Use carefully:** Differentiate resident receipts from all workplace payments.

**Example:** Only payments reaching resident workers and owners enter resident income.

### income gain
A change in income under a stated measure.

**Use carefully:** Distinguish nominal income from real purchasing power.

**Example:** Nominal income rises, but the basket-cost increase offsets part of the gain.

## Units

### log points
A scaled logarithmic difference under an explicitly stated convention.

**Use carefully:** Declare whether the reported unit is 100 times a log change.

**Example:** The table reports 100 times the log difference between July and January income.

### percentage points
An arithmetic difference between percentages.

**Use carefully:** Do not use for a proportional percentage change.

**Example:** The resident ownership share rises by two percentage points.

### percent change
A relative arithmetic change, multiplied by one hundred.

**Use carefully:** For finite changes it differs from a scaled log difference.

**Example:** The percent change is computed from the ratio of final to initial expenditure.

### initial-income points
A monetary change divided by initial income and scaled as declared.

**Use carefully:** Do not automatically label this a log-welfare change.

**Example:** The basket-cost increase is expressed in points of initial income.

### per resident
A total divided by the declared resident population.

**Use carefully:** Keep denominator dates and support consistent.

**Example:** Monthly income per resident uses the baseline resident count.

### per area
A total divided by geographic area.

**Use carefully:** Specify area units and treatment of water or uninhabited land.

**Example:** Spending density is euros per square kilometer.

### level
A quantity measured in its original or declared normalized units.

**Use carefully:** Differentiate a common level from relative spatial variation.

**Example:** Time effects absorb the citywide level shift.

### relative change
A change measured against a stated comparison object.

**Use carefully:** Specify the omitted location, period, or numeraire.

**Example:** The coefficient compares the tract with other origins in the same month.

### elasticity
A proportional response to a proportional change in a specified variable.

**Use carefully:** State the margin, horizon, conditioning, and sign convention.

**Example:** The elasticity describes commuting's response to wages holding residence fixed.

### semi-elasticity
A proportional response to a unit change, or the declared converse.

**Use carefully:** State which variable is logged.

**Example:** The semi-elasticity is a log-price response to a one-point exposure increase.

### numeraire
The price or value used to anchor nominal units.

**Use carefully:** Normalization does not supply new identifying variation.

**Example:** The outside good is the numeraire before and after the shock.

### normalization
A restriction fixing scale or location of otherwise equivalent representations.

**Use carefully:** Explain what remains unidentified.

**Example:** Mean-zero taste shocks fix the scale of the recovered index.

## Theory

### primitives
Preferences, technology, endowments, and other starting objects.

**Use carefully:** Do not call equilibrium outcomes primitives.

**Example:** The primitives include location amenities and the transport-cost matrix.

### preferences
The objective governing household choices.

**Use carefully:** Name the restrictions actually used.

**Example:** The valuation result begins with general preferences.

### technology
The feasible transformation of inputs into outputs.

**Use carefully:** Separate technological restrictions from market structure.

**Example:** The firm has a general production function with a flexible material input.

### timing
The order in which information and choices occur.

**Use carefully:** State when prices are observed and what cannot adjust.

**Example:** Workplace assignments precede the seasonal demand realization.

### fixed input
An input constrained not to adjust in the exercise.

**Use carefully:** Fixed quantity does not imply fixed payment.

**Example:** Labor quantity is fixed, but the return to workplace labor can change.

### flexible input
An input chosen after the relevant change.

**Use carefully:** Its input price may or may not be fixed.

**Example:** Materials adjust at the given outside price.

### optimization
Choice of the best feasible plan under an objective.

**Use carefully:** State both objective and constraints.

**Example:** The household minimizes expenditure needed for its initial utility.

### optimality condition
A necessary or sufficient condition for an optimum under stated regularity.

**Use carefully:** Do not silently treat a necessary condition as globally sufficient.

**Example:** The input condition equates marginal revenue with marginal input cost.

### equilibrium
A collection of choices and prices satisfying the model's conditions.

**Use carefully:** List the conditions rather than saying markets equilibrate.

**Example:** Equilibrium requires optimal choices, market clearing, and budget consistency.

### market clearing
Equality of supplied and demanded quantities on a specified market.

**Use carefully:** Keep quantities distinct from payment identities.

**Example:** Workplace labor demand equals the effective labor supplied by commuters.

### closure
The restrictions completing the counterfactual system.

**Use carefully:** Specify outside resources, mobility, ownership, and aggregate constraints.

**Example:** The open-city closure fixes outside utility and permits migration.

### partial equilibrium
A response holding specified other objects fixed.

**Use carefully:** Say precisely what is held fixed.

**Example:** The partial-equilibrium comparison holds destination prices unchanged.

### general equilibrium
Joint adjustment across the model's linked markets.

**Use carefully:** The phrase does not guarantee every relevant margin is included.

**Example:** Wages, spending, and service prices adjust jointly in this equilibrium.

### existence
There is at least one object satisfying the declared conditions.

**Use carefully:** Does not imply uniqueness or numerical convergence.

**Example:** The proposition establishes existence of a positive equilibrium.

### uniqueness
Only one admissible solution exists under stated conditions.

**Use carefully:** Distinguish equilibrium uniqueness from unique recovery of fundamentals.

**Example:** Observed shares uniquely recover fundamentals for the fixed parameter vector.

### invertibility
A mapping permits unique recovery of its argument on the relevant domain.

**Use carefully:** Check normalization, domain, and support.

**Example:** Demand inversion recovers relative attractiveness from positive shares.

### convergence
A sequence approaches its limiting object under declared conditions.

**Use carefully:** Nonsingularity alone does not establish convergence of a feedback expansion.

**Example:** The series converges because the operator's spectral radius is below one.

### stability
An explicitly defined response to perturbations.

**Use carefully:** Specify dynamic, local, or algorithmic stability.

**Example:** Local stability refers to small migration deviations from the equilibrium.

### path dependence
Temporary history can permanently change the long-run outcome.

**Use carefully:** Persistence alone is insufficient.

**Example:** The temporary shock can select a different steady state.

### persistence
Effects decay slowly or carry over through time.

**Use carefully:** Do not presume permanent effects.

**Example:** The response persists but converges back to the original steady state.

### CES
A constant-elasticity substitution specification.

**Use carefully:** A common elasticity does not eliminate all origin-specific access changes.

**Example:** CES is introduced for the response-recovery step, not the initial envelope.

### IIA
A specified invariance of relative choice responses to alternatives.

**Use carefully:** State the relevant choices and local or global form.

**Example:** The maintained local IIA restriction makes destination relative-share responses common across origins.

### Fréchet distribution
An extreme-value distribution used for specified heterogeneous draws.

**Use carefully:** Shape, choice probability, and effective-labor exponents need not coincide.

**Example:** The shape parameter governs workplace-choice responsiveness in this calibration.

### effective labor
Labor services after the declared productivity or selection adjustment.

**Use carefully:** Do not substitute headcounts without conversion.

**Example:** Wage bills are matched to effective labor rather than worker counts alone.

### hours elasticity
The hours response to a stated incentive.

**Use carefully:** Workplace mobility does not imply elastic hours.

**Example:** Hours remain fixed while workplace choices adjust.

### iceberg cost
A wedge requiring extra units shipped for one unit delivered.

**Use carefully:** Tax revenue is not automatically represented by an iceberg loss.

**Example:** The transport wedge reduces delivered quantity without generating rebate revenue.

### agglomeration
A force making concentration increase productivity or attractiveness.

**Use carefully:** Distinguish a reduced-form force from its identified microfoundation.

**Example:** Higher workplace density raises the modeled productivity component.

### dispersion force
A force discouraging concentration.

**Use carefully:** Name the relevant cost or preference margin.

**Example:** Rising land prices discourage further residential concentration.

### externality
An effect on others not internalized in the specified choice.

**Use carefully:** A network linkage need not itself be an externality.

**Example:** Congestion imposes a travel cost on other road users.

### fundamentals
Declared exogenous location characteristics.

**Use carefully:** Recovered composites can contain more than one primitive.

**Example:** Recovered productivity combines the components the data cannot separate.

## Identification

### identification
Uniqueness of the economic or statistical object implied by observations and assumptions.

**Use carefully:** An estimator's existence does not establish identification.

**Example:** Orthogonality and rank conditions identify the response coefficient.

### exogeneity
A specified independence or orthogonality condition relative to the error.

**Use carefully:** Name the error and conditioning set.

**Example:** The shock is assumed orthogonal to unobserved origin demand after controls.

### endogeneity
Dependence that invalidates the stated estimating restriction.

**Use carefully:** Describe its economic source.

**Example:** Amenities move visitor and resident spending together.

### interference
One unit's outcome depends on another unit's treatment or shock.

**Use carefully:** Untreated does not necessarily mean unexposed.

**Example:** A tract receives income through workplaces in tourist destinations.

### spillover
An effect reaching another unit through a specified channel.

**Use carefully:** Separate causal spillovers from constructed exposure.

**Example:** The model predicts spending spillovers through resident income.

### instrument
A variable used under relevance and exclusion restrictions.

**Use carefully:** Controls and instruments require different arguments.

**Example:** Predicted foreign demand shifts instrument local export exposure.

### exclusion restriction
A declared restriction removing a direct or correlated path to the outcome error.

**Use carefully:** Do not imply it is proven by first-stage strength.

**Example:** The instrument affects local spending only through the modeled demand shift.

### orthogonality
A zero moment between specified objects under the maintained model.

**Use carefully:** Identify the expectation, error, and information set.

**Example:** The residual is orthogonal to the exogenous policy shifter.

### rank condition
Variation sufficient to distinguish the targeted coefficients or objects.

**Use carefully:** Validity without rank does not identify a response.

**Example:** Residualized direct and network exposures must supply independent variation.

### fixed effects
Indicators absorbing common variation within declared groups.

**Use carefully:** Absorption does not separately recover each economic component.

**Example:** Origin-month effects combine income and basket-related changes.

### normalization sensitivity
Dependence of results on the restriction anchoring an unidentified scale or level.

**Use carefully:** Not necessarily sampling uncertainty.

**Example:** Alternative taste normalizations change the aggregate price-index level.

### income recovery
Inference of income changes from measured outcomes and a maintained system.

**Use carefully:** Distinguish observed income from recovered income.

**Example:** Recovered income uses spending responses and a basket restriction.

### basket adjustment
A specified weighted correction for destination terms in origin spending.

**Use carefully:** State weights, terms, and imposed or estimated coefficients.

**Example:** Both destination components are netted using initial resident shares.

### shift-share
Exposure formed by combining specified baseline shares and shocks.

**Use carefully:** State shock validity, share timing, support, and dependence.

**Example:** Initial tourist-group shares weight external group demand changes.

### predictable variation
Variation explained by a declared forecasting or calendar construction.

**Use carefully:** Do not label fitted future information predetermined.

**Example:** Calendar controls remove predictable group-specific seasonality.

### residualization
Removal of projections on a specified set of controls.

**Use carefully:** It changes the variation being plotted or estimated.

**Example:** The scatter uses flows residualized on origin and destination effects.

### pre-trend
A pattern before treatment relevant to the stated design.

**Use carefully:** Non-rejection does not prove parallel counterfactual trends.

**Example:** The pre-treatment coefficients are imprecise and centered near zero.

### placebo
A comparison designed to probe a particular spurious explanation.

**Use carefully:** State the threat; passing one placebo does not validate the entire design.

**Example:** Unbuilt routes probe selection into proposed infrastructure.

## Data

### support
The units or values over which the object is observed or recovered.

**Use carefully:** Do not silently compare different supports.

**Example:** All three specifications use the same positive-flow origin-destination pairs.

### crosswalk
A documented mapping between classifications or geographic units.

**Use carefully:** Check many-to-many weights and unmatched records.

**Example:** The crosswalk assigns merchant records to constant-boundary tracts.

### coverage
The observed share of the target population or activity.

**Use carefully:** A large dataset need not be representative.

**Example:** The resident series requires an observed home address.

### imputation
Replacement of missing values under a declared procedure.

**Use carefully:** Distinguish imputed links from observed links.

**Example:** Commuting links are imputed using the stated gravity model.

### scaling
A transformation changing units or matching an aggregate.

**Use carefully:** Disclose the target total and whether results use raw or scaled data.

**Example:** Provider expenditure is scaled to the baseline account total.

### retention
The declared share of payments remaining with the target residents or owners.

**Use carefully:** Average retention need not equal marginal retention.

**Example:** The account allocates the maintained resident share of nonlabor returns.

### outside resources
Income or demand entering from outside the modeled system.

**Use carefully:** Specify which components can respond to the shock.

**Example:** Outside income is held fixed in the seasonal counterfactual.

### autonomous demand
Demand not generated by modeled resident-income feedback.

**Use carefully:** Do not confuse it with an identified exogenous shock.

**Example:** Autonomous demand closes the baseline sales account.

## Networks

### baseline shares
Allocation weights in the initial observed or constructed account.

**Use carefully:** They are not automatically response derivatives.

**Example:** Initial shopping shares allocate the constructed demand exposure.

### marginal allocation
The distribution of an incremental payment or resource change.

**Use carefully:** It may differ from average baseline allocation.

**Example:** Marginal income payments use the maintained allocation matrix.

### exposure
A declared measure of potential contact with a shock.

**Use carefully:** Exposure is not an estimated effect.

**Example:** Network exposure traces demand through baseline spending and income links.

### transmission
The mechanism carrying responses through the economy.

**Use carefully:** Distinguish equilibrium transmission from exposure construction.

**Example:** Prices and incomes jointly determine equilibrium transmission.

### propagation round
One application of a specified feedback operation.

**Use carefully:** State the held-fixed assumptions.

**Example:** The next round spends the income allocated from the previous sales increase.

### leakage
A payment or purchase leaving the modeled recirculation system.

**Use carefully:** Scalar leakage alone may not establish convergence.

**Example:** Outside purchases limit the income spent at modeled destinations.

### multiplier
An aggregate or local response ratio under specified conditions.

**Use carefully:** Name numerator, denominator, and weights.

**Example:** The sales multiplier divides total constructed exposure by the direct demand increase.

### spectral radius
The largest absolute eigenvalue of a matrix.

**Use carefully:** State which operator is measured and why it matters.

**Example:** The baseline propagation matrix has a spectral radius below one.

### market access
A model-specific aggregation of links to economic activity elsewhere.

**Use carefully:** Do not interchange inward, outward, commuter, and consumption access.

**Example:** Commuter access weights reachable workplace opportunities.

### commuting openness
A declared measure of cross-boundary commuting.

**Use carefully:** Residence and workplace denominators differ.

**Example:** The residence measure counts residents working outside their tract.

## Estimation

### observation unit
The entity indexed by one row in the analysis.

**Use carefully:** A location-month is not a bilateral-pair-month.

**Example:** Each observation is an origin-destination-month spending flow.

### estimator
The stated rule mapping data into an estimate.

**Use carefully:** Distinguish estimator assumptions from economic assumptions.

**Example:** The IV estimator uses residualized instruments and controls.

### estimand
The population quantity targeted by the estimator.

**Use carefully:** Do not define it only by a software command.

**Example:** The estimand is the direct response conditional on network exposure.

### coefficient
A parameter in the estimating specification.

**Use carefully:** Its economic interpretation depends on the model and units.

**Example:** The destination coefficient enters the recovered sales response.

### standard error
A measure of sampling uncertainty for an estimator under stated conditions.

**Use carefully:** Not a robustness range.

**Example:** Parentheses contain two-way clustered standard errors.

### confidence interval
An interval constructed by a stated repeated-sampling procedure.

**Use carefully:** A conditional interval need not include calibration uncertainty.

**Example:** The interval holds the ownership allocation fixed.

### clustering
An inference procedure allowing dependence within declared groups.

**Use carefully:** Specify dimensions and cluster counts where relevant.

**Example:** Errors are clustered by origin and month.

### weighting
The relative contribution assigned to observations.

**Use carefully:** Weights change the target comparison or estimator.

**Example:** The comparison uses initial resident population weights.

### trimming
Excluding observations under a specified rule.

**Use carefully:** Report the support change and avoid outcome-driven selection.

**Example:** The sensitivity excludes origins below the predefined coverage threshold.

### winsorization
Replacing tail values with declared thresholds.

**Use carefully:** It is not the same as dropping tails.

**Example:** Weights above the stated percentile are capped.

### pooled sample
Observations from declared groups or periods analyzed together.

**Use carefully:** Common coefficients may impose a substantive restriction.

**Example:** The pooled regression estimates common responses for both years.

### generated regressor
A regressor constructed using an earlier estimated object.

**Use carefully:** Ordinary standard errors may omit its uncertainty.

**Example:** The network control uses predicted spending from the first-stage model.

### smearing
A declared correction for predicting levels from a log specification.

**Use carefully:** Exponentiating a fitted log alone generally differs from a conditional mean in levels.

**Example:** Level predictions use the pooled residual-smearing factor.

### moment condition
An expectation restriction used in estimation or testing.

**Use carefully:** Targeted and untargeted moments have different evidentiary roles.

**Example:** The moment sets instrument-weighted prediction errors to zero.

## Validation

### robustness
Stability of a named conclusion under specified changes.

**Use carefully:** Name sign, ordering, magnitude, support, and tested scope.

**Example:** The spatial ordering survives, but the mean changes sign.

### sensitivity
Dependence on a specified assumption or parameter.

**Use carefully:** Not automatically a confidence bound.

**Example:** The recovery sensitivity changes the basket coefficient restriction.

### model fit
Agreement with specified measured objects under a metric.

**Use carefully:** Exact baseline matching can be mechanical.

**Example:** The model fits untargeted commuting changes better than the restricted variant.

### validation
Evidence bearing on a specific model use or prediction.

**Use carefully:** Do not claim universal validity from one test.

**Example:** The test evaluates the seasonal response, not every policy counterfactual.

### untargeted moment
A statistic not used in the stated estimation or calibration target.

**Use carefully:** Check overlap and mechanical informativeness.

**Example:** The comparison uses an income response not targeted by the calibration.

### out-of-sample
Evaluation on observations not used for the specified fitting task.

**Use carefully:** Name the split and any shared preprocessing.

**Example:** Parameters fitted in the early period predict later trade flows.

### non-rejection
A test does not reject its null at a declared level.

**Use carefully:** Not confirmation; discuss precision and power.

**Example:** We cannot reject the predicted slope, but the interval is wide.

### power
A test's ability to reject specified alternatives.

**Use carefully:** Specify alternatives relevant to the economic question.

**Example:** The simulation examines power against welfare-relevant misspecification.

### mechanical fit
Agreement induced by fitting choices rather than independent evidence.

**Use carefully:** Disclose which moments determine the compared output.

**Example:** The account identity guarantees this baseline match.

### misspecification
A departure from the maintained model relevant to an object or test.

**Use carefully:** Say which restriction or prediction fails.

**Example:** The residual correlation indicates misspecification of the response equation.

## Quantitative

### counterfactual
An outcome under an explicitly changed environment and maintained conditions.

**Use carefully:** Declare shock, baseline, closure, and unchanged objects.

**Example:** The counterfactual removes the tax while preserving outside resources.

### calibration
Selection of parameters or fundamentals to match declared targets.

**Use carefully:** Not every calibrated object is empirically identified.

**Example:** The labor parameter is calibrated from an external estimate.

### inversion
Recovery of latent objects from a model mapping and observations.

**Use carefully:** State parameters, normalization, and uniqueness conditions.

**Example:** The inversion recovers relative productivity conditional on the elasticities.

### exact hat algebra
A change representation eliminating certain level fundamentals under a specific model.

**Use carefully:** Exact refers to that maintained model, not universal empirical truth.

**Example:** The hat system computes finite changes without re-estimating every initial fundamental.

### Jacobian
A matrix of derivatives at a specified point.

**Use carefully:** Check dimensions, normalization, and evaluation point.

**Example:** The Jacobian maps local demand changes into equilibrium responses.

### residual
A difference from a declared equation or fitted value.

**Use carefully:** Distinguish statistical residuals from equilibrium residuals and economic amenities.

**Example:** The returned state has small market-clearing residuals.

### nesting
A specification contains another under stated parameter restrictions.

**Use carefully:** Verify the limit; do not assume it from similar equations.

**Example:** The fixed-workplace case is recovered in the specified limiting experiment.

### nonlinear response
The finite response obtained from a declared nonlinear system.

**Use carefully:** Separate it from re-estimation at a new endpoint.

**Example:** The nonlinear solve holds estimated parameters fixed.

### bound
A valid restriction on an unknown object under specified conditions.

**Use carefully:** An interval between two preferred estimates is not automatically a bound.

**Example:** The finite welfare bound uses the stated expenditure inequalities.

### feasibility
Compatibility with the full set of declared constraints.

**Use carefully:** Small unscaled residuals may not establish meaningful feasibility.

**Example:** The endpoint fails the producer inequalities on the original scale.

### endpoint
A specified initial or final state in a finite-change exercise.

**Use carefully:** Distinguish observed, projected, and separately fitted endpoints.

**Example:** The projected final endpoint is reported separately from the observed account.

## Policy

### policy premium
The additional welfare under one feasible policy relative to another.

**Use carefully:** Give the base, units, and optimization status.

**Example:** The district policy's gain is measured relative to the uniform-tax optimum.

### tax base
The value or quantity to which a tax rate applies.

**Use carefully:** Gross consumer payments and producer receipts can differ.

**Example:** The tax applies to producer-price tourist spending.

### rebate
The specified redistribution of collected revenue.

**Use carefully:** Identify recipients, weights, and equilibrium feedback.

**Example:** Revenue is rebated equally to resident households.

### incidence
The distribution of economic gains or losses from a change.

**Use carefully:** Tax remittance does not determine economic incidence.

**Example:** Visitors remit the tax, but resident welfare depends on prices and income.

### KKT conditions
Necessary optimality conditions under appropriate constraint qualifications.

**Use carefully:** They do not by themselves establish global optimality in a nonconvex problem.

**Example:** The candidate satisfies the reported boundary and gradient checks.

### local optimum
An admissible policy not improved by nearby feasible changes.

**Use carefully:** Do not call it the global optimum without further evidence.

**Example:** Different starts reach near-equivalent local solutions.

### global optimum
The best admissible solution over the full feasible set.

**Use carefully:** Numerical convergence alone is insufficient.

**Example:** The reported solve does not establish global optimality.

### data-based
A calculation using declared observed or estimated inputs.

**Use carefully:** Do not call it data-only when calibration or equilibrium restrictions remain.

**Example:** The approximation uses estimated responses and a maintained rebate rule.

## Claims

### shows
Introduces an established display, derivation, or empirical pattern.

**Use carefully:** Specify what is shown and avoid implying stronger causal evidence.

**Example:** The figure shows a wider dispersion in commuting openness.

### suggests
Signals limited evidence for an interpretation.

**Use carefully:** Name the evidence and credible alternatives.

**Example:** The contrast suggests an earnings channel but does not separate ownership.

### implies
States a consequence of premises.

**Use carefully:** Identify the relevant assumption or model.

**Example:** The fixed-input restriction implies no labor-quantity adjustment in this exercise.

### estimates
Reports a statistical estimate of a declared object.

**Use carefully:** Does not prove exogeneity.

**Example:** The regression estimates the response conditional on predictable controls.

### predicts
Reports a model implication.

**Use carefully:** Distinguish prediction from observation.

**Example:** The model predicts higher spending at connected destinations.

### identifies
Asserts a unique object under stated observational and economic restrictions.

**Use carefully:** Requires more than a coefficient from a fitted regression.

**Example:** The rank condition identifies the two response coefficients under orthogonality.

### is consistent with
The evidence does not contradict a specified explanation.

**Use carefully:** Does not exclude alternatives.

**Example:** The spatial pattern is consistent with income received through commuting links.

### accounts for
Explains or quantitatively matches a declared object.

**Use carefully:** State metric and whether matching was targeted.

**Example:** The simulated response accounts for part of the observed spatial variation.

### holds fixed
Names an object excluded from adjustment.

**Use carefully:** Avoid all else equal without listing economically relevant objects.

**Example:** The mobility comparison holds recovered income and sales responses fixed.

### under
Attaches a condition directly to a claim.

**Use carefully:** Use a named assumption if the condition is long.

**Example:** Under common substitution responses, the destination component is shared across origins.

## Transitions

### because
States the reason for a consequence.

**Use carefully:** Check that the reason is established, not merely plausible.

**Example:** Income must be recovered because the transaction records do not directly observe it.

### therefore
Marks a conclusion following from the preceding argument.

**Use carefully:** Do not use to leap from correlation to causation.

**Example:** The price components remain mixed, so the origin effect is not yet income.

### however
Marks a consequential contrast.

**Use carefully:** Name the dimension that changes.

**Example:** The ordering is stable; however, the mean becomes negative.

### in turn
Marks a dependent next step.

**Use carefully:** Avoid as filler for unrelated sequence.

**Example:** Recovered sales changes, in turn, supply the price-matching variation.

### to recover
Introduces the purpose of an operation.

**Use carefully:** Name the required object and restriction.

**Example:** To recover relative prices, we invert the stated demand system.

### this distinction
Carries a specific preceding contrast forward.

**Use carefully:** Avoid when several distinctions could be meant.

**Example:** This distinction separates fixed-response valuation from equilibrium adjustment.

### the remaining question
Identifies the next unresolved issue.

**Use carefully:** Do not restate the whole roadmap.

**Example:** The remaining question is whether the responses recover the aggregate income level.

## Plain-Language

### framework
An organized collection of model objects and relationships.

**Use carefully:** Name those objects; do not ban the word or use it instead of substance.

**Example:** The framework combines consumer valuation, producer returns, and network transmission.

### tractable
A stated analytical or computational task becomes feasible.

**Use carefully:** Say what can now be derived or solved.

**Example:** The restriction yields a tractable system with a closed-form demand response.

### rich
Contains specified variation or dimensions.

**Use carefully:** Replace with the actual dimension when that is more informative.

**Example:** The data record many destinations and repeated purchases by the same origins.

### novel
Claims a particular departure from previous work.

**Use carefully:** Describe the departure instead of relying on novelty alone.

**Example:** We add intermediate shopping stops to the commuting model.

### leverage
Uses an input or source of variation.

**Use carefully:** Prefer use unless the operation needs a more specific verb.

**Example:** We use external demand changes to construct the instrument.

### unpack
Explains components of an object.

**Use carefully:** Prefer explain, decompose, or interpret when the operation is known.

**Example:** We decompose the change into income and basket costs.

### robustly
Claims stability across a stated scope.

**Use carefully:** Replace with the actual checked comparison.

**Example:** The sign remains positive in all three declared weighting checks.

### meaningful
Claims economic or statistical relevance.

**Use carefully:** Provide a magnitude or decision criterion.

**Example:** The change is large relative to the initial monthly income measure.

### holistic
Claims broad coverage.

**Use carefully:** List the markets or margins rather than using this adjective.

**Example:** The comparison includes wages, service prices, and rebate spending.

### nuanced
Claims a more differentiated interpretation.

**Use carefully:** State the actual difference.

**Example:** Lower-income tracts gain more proportionally but less in euros.

### underscores
Emphasizes a conclusion.

**Use carefully:** Usually state the conclusion directly.

**Example:** The sign reversal makes the recovery restriction central to the aggregate result.

### reconcile
Explains or resolves a specific disagreement.

**Use carefully:** Distinguish explanation from forcing numerical agreement.

**Example:** Different denominators explain the apparent discrepancy between the two series.

### data-driven
Uses data in some declared way.

**Use carefully:** Specify what is estimated and what remains calibrated.

**Example:** The tax ranking uses estimated local responses and calibrated substitution parameters.

### comprehensive
Claims complete coverage of a declared scope.

**Use carefully:** Define the scope and report gaps.

**Example:** The exhibit audit covers every active manuscript figure and table.
