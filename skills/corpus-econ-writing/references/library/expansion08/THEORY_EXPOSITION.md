# Theory Exposition: Conditions, Derivations, and Interpretation

## Begin With The Economic Question

A theory section should explain what must be learned before listing the
conditions needed to learn it. Introduce agents, choices, timing, constraints,
units, and the outside option when they first matter. A reader should understand
the purpose of a restriction before encountering its formal statement.

Do not turn this into a requirement that every paragraph have the same shape.
An example, a proof, a comparison, and an empirical handoff serve different jobs.
Use the smallest sequence that makes the inference intelligible.

## Put Conditions At The Point Of Use

Separate three layers: primitives defining the economy; conditions sufficient
for local valuation; and stronger restrictions needed for recovery, aggregation,
or finite changes. An envelope formula does not by itself identify income from
purchases, establish a convergent feedback expansion, or bound a large shock.

For a consequential assumption:

1. Explain the obstacle: which observed response can have more than one cause?
2. State the condition and its scope. Distinguish a maintained restriction from a
   theorem conclusion or an empirically tested implication.
3. Cite the condition when using it in a proposition or derivation.
4. Interpret what becomes recoverable and give a relevant failure case.

Avoid an early inventory repeated at each later step. Equally, do not postpone a
condition until after using it. Keep required assumptions in the argument;
secondary ancestry and detailed proof navigation can go in footnotes.

## Show The Step That Does The Work

Definitions alone do not explain a theorem. After stating an optimized value,
show the cancellation, substitution, accounting equality, or matrix equation
that connects the premises to the claimed object. State what remains fixed and
which choice has already been optimized. Interpret the resulting derivative in
economic units before proceeding.

For production, distinguish input quantities chosen after the shock from input
prices assumed unchanged. If an input price changes, retain its envelope term.
For households, distinguish a fixed feasible set from a parameterized constraint.
A discrete choice can support an envelope at an appropriate differentiability
point; this does not mean changes in the available choices never matter.

For a ratio, explain numerator and denominator separately. A marginal price
share is not generally an average income share. Specify whether a differential
is a level change, a proportional change, or a contribution normalized by
initial income. A small-change identity need not hold for a finite log change.

For a fixed-point system, distinguish a nonsingular inverse from a convergent
round-by-round expansion. Specify the additional convergence condition. If a
regularity condition remains unresolved, disclose it rather than improve the
prose by asserting it.

## Partial And Total Policy Derivatives

Define the equilibrium system, policy instrument, transfer rule, and welfare
objective before taking derivatives. For every consequential partial derivative,
say which arguments are fixed. A direct transfer value holds the equilibrium
state fixed; a total transfer value includes the equilibrium response.

Choose one complete chain rule. Either use partial welfare derivatives and
explicit equilibrium/transfer responses, or use a previously defined total
derivative that already includes those responses. Do not multiply an already
total transfer value by feedback and then add the same feedback again.

Include explicit policy dependence in welfare when present. Show the denominator
or Jacobian that makes the implicit response well defined; nonsingularity is not
a stability or global-optimality claim. A first-order condition is not proof of
a unique policy optimum. These distinctions apply to transport revenue recycling
as well as spatial or visitor taxes, without importing a source model's labor-tax
distortion into a model that does not contain it.

## Interpret Without Repeating

Use the interpretation to explain who gains, through which payment or purchase,
why the sign can change, and what the proposition leaves unresolved. Do not just
translate every symbol into a sentence. A useful counterexample changes a stated
condition and shows which inference fails, while preserving unrelated results.

A paragraph should inherit an object or conclusion from its predecessor. A
subsection should supply something used by the next: valuation needs responses;
recovery needs restrictions and data; estimation needs identifying variation.
When the next step changes the object, say so explicitly. Do not create a smooth
transition by implying a stronger identification claim.

## Organize Literature Around The Task

Choose the economic question first: valuation, unequal incidence, identifying
behavior, or endogenous spatial adjustment. Compare studies within that task
through their restrictions, objects, and implications. Do not write one summary
paragraph per author when the requested purpose is a comparison.

An outline such as "Study A does X; Study B does Y; Study C does Z" is a planning
device, not a completed literature discussion. Connect the studies: explain why
the second object requires more structure than the first, or why an alternative
data source answers a different question. Never present precedent as validation
of the current design or compare model-dependent percentages without compatible
objects and denominators.

## Final Pass

Check publication prose for writer-facing commands: "state the restriction here,"
"add interpretation," "move this to the appendix," and similar editing language
belong in a review, not a manuscript. Preserve substantive exposition when
repairing these passages. Review equations and conditions independently of the
vocabulary gate. Report self-review as self-review; an improved score after
seeing a reference key is not independent validation.
