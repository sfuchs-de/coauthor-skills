# Reviewer-Style Markdown Reports

Use this style for the default Markdown deliverable or for a report modeled on actual reviewer examples.

## Format

- Write Markdown, not LaTeX.
- Use a simple title such as `# Report on "[Title]"` or `# Reviewer report on "[Title]"`.
- Use `## Summary`, `## Comments`, and optionally `## Smaller comments` for initial reports.
- Use `## Assessment of the revision`, `## Remaining comments`, and optionally
  `## Smaller comments` for revision rounds.
- Use numbered comments. Subcomments like `1.1` and `1.2` are fine when they make the report easier to read.
- Keep minor comments short. Lettered items (`a.`, `b.`, `c.`) are acceptable.
- Use concise mode by default: 750-1,000 words and two or three major comments. Use the standard
  900-1,200-word range only for explicitly comprehensive reports or several independent defects.
- Keep revision reports to 400-700 words.

## Voice

- Sound like a human referee, not a report generator.
- First-person judgment is allowed: `I liked the paper`, `I struggled to see`, `It was not clear to me`, `I was hoping to see`.
- Preserve specific first-person reactions when they communicate evaluation. Do not mechanically
  rewrite them as repeated `the paper should` or `the authors should` instructions.
- Keep praise concrete and brief. Say what is interesting or well done, then move to the binding concerns.
- Prefer plain verbs and direct requests: `show`, `clarify`, `discuss`, `move`, `separate`, `explain`.
- Avoid title-case issue labels unless they are genuinely helpful. Parenthetical labels such as `(First stage)` or `(Identification)` are enough.
- Put the objection in the first sentence of a major comment. Do not spend a paragraph reintroducing
  the relevant section before saying what is wrong.
- End with one natural request such as `I think the paper needs to...`; do not display a separate
  `Suggestion` or `Resolution threshold` label in an ordinary report.

## Evidence Discipline

- Preserve the skill's normal evidence rules. Do not invent facts, tables, sample sizes, coefficients, institutional details, or citations.
- Keep anchors sparse and unobtrusive. One section, table, figure, or appendix anchor per paragraph is usually enough.
- Do not include page anchors merely to prove diligence. Use page anchors for exact quotes, typos, or hard-to-find claims.
- If a concern is conceptual rather than manuscript-specific, write it as the reviewer's reaction rather than as an established manuscript fact.

## Style Targets

- The opening should combine summary and judgment in two to four paragraphs.
- Include a concise contribution benchmark in that opening. Name the closest relevant work or the
  established margin and explain what remains new; do not defer all positioning to a minor comment.
- The main comments should contain the anchored claim, threat, consequence, one primary request,
  and a resolution threshold while reading as natural referee notes rather than a template.
- It is acceptable for comments to be slightly asymmetrical: one long central comment, one medium comment, and a few smaller comments often reads more like real reviews than five equally weighted blocks.
- Use concrete phrases such as `the core treatment`, `the key result`, `the story of the paper`, and `what I would like to see`.
- Avoid mechanical transitions, repeated `this matters because`, over-neat taxonomies, and dense literature-positioning unless the user asks for that.
- Treat alternative diagnostics as substitutes and do not repeat major-comment remedies under
  smaller comments.
- Keep the investigation deeper than the report. One decisive equation, estimate, or institutional
  fact is usually stronger than an inventory of every related concern.
- After the major comments, retain a few inexpensive corrections involving definitions, units,
  notation, aggregation, or figure readability when they would materially help a revision. A single
  broad exposition comment should not displace all concrete minor fixes.
