# Result-First Technical Exposition

Use this protocol when a technically correct economics paper feels difficult because the reader
must hold too many unresolved branches at once. The objective is not uniformly shorter prose. It
is a recoverable argument whose central result, economic contrast, and formal support arrive in the
order the reader needs them.

Treat the protocol as a diagnostic default, not a house style. Preserve a stronger local structure,
the author's voice, and field or outlet conventions.

## Compress through ordering

At the paper level, prefer the following sequence when the material supports it:

1. Pose one concrete economic question or puzzle.
2. State the leading competing intuitions only when they genuinely exist.
3. Give the paper's answer, sufficient statistic, proposition, or empirical result early.
4. Translate the answer into one memorable economic distinction.
5. Test the distinction with a polar, limiting, or otherwise diagnostic case.
6. State the empirical or quantitative implementation and headline magnitudes.
7. Position the result in the literature after the reader understands it.
8. Add generalizations, qualifications, and machinery in dependency order.

Do not manufacture a debate to fill step 2, display an equation merely for emphasis, or move a
result forward before the necessary object and scope can be stated accurately.

## Apply the same architecture recursively

Use a smaller version of the sequence at each scale:

- **Section:** section contract -> simplest informative case -> formal object -> interpretation ->
  boundary case -> generalization or handoff.
- **Subsection:** question -> answer -> support -> implication.
- **Paragraph:** topic claim -> evidence or formula -> economic mechanism -> qualification or next
  edge.

The prose should expose the reasoning graph. A reader should be able to identify what each unit
inherits, what it establishes, and what the next unit may now use.

## Build an exposition packet around a central equation

For a load-bearing equation or sufficient statistic:

1. Ask the exact economic question the equation answers.
2. Introduce the economic object before displaying notation.
3. Display only the version needed at this point in the argument.
4. Define each term immediately and in the order it appears.
5. Restate the expression in plain economic language.
6. Identify the sign, margin, contrast, or mechanism doing the work.
7. Give a zero-effect, polar, or limiting case when it reveals the result.
8. State the approximation order, equilibrium closure, or omitted force near the result rather
   than hiding it in a remote footnote.
9. Defer the full derivation or generalization only after providing a precise handoff.

Do not call a formula intuitive when the prose has not supplied the intuition. Do not alter the
equation, theorem scope, or evidentiary claim merely to make the packet cleaner.

## Keep one contrast and one vocabulary

Choose the smallest stable set of terms that makes the mechanism memorable. Reuse those exact
terms in the abstract, introduction, model interpretation, results, and conclusion. Good contrasts
often distinguish the economic objects readers would otherwise conflate: stocks versus flows,
holdings versus transactions, direct versus equilibrium effects, or exposure versus incidence.

Intentional repetition of the core result is useful when each occurrence does a different job:

- the abstract states it;
- the introduction explains and scopes it;
- the body establishes and quantifies it;
- the conclusion interprets it.

Avoid synonym drift, repeated launches, and mechanically identical restatements.

## Write results as claim-mechanism packets

For each headline result, prefer:

1. the directional claim;
2. the relevant magnitude and unit;
3. the benchmark, distribution, or counterfactual that gives it meaning;
4. the mechanism or decomposition;
5. the scope condition or important exception.

Use sequencing words such as *first* and *second* only when the enumeration helps readers retain a
small number of main findings. Do not use a numbered list to conceal an unclear hierarchy.

## Make dense material skimmable

- Use informative section contracts and, where appropriate, restrained run-in labels such as
  **Individual problem**, **Welfare effect**, or **Empirical implementation**.
- Let each paragraph perform one recognizable job even when sentences must be technically dense.
- Put nonessential citations, algebra, and documentation in footnotes or appendices; keep load-
  bearing qualifications in the main text.
- Give tables and figures argumentative captions and discuss the comparison that matters.
- Prefer conventional syntax and stable terminology over ornamental transitions.

Run-in labels are navigation, not decoration. Avoid so many labels that the paper reads like a
manual or fragments a continuous argument.

## Audit questions

Before finishing, ask:

- Can a reader recover the question, answer, mechanism, and scope before encountering secondary
  machinery?
- Is the central contrast economically correct and expressed with stable terms?
- Does every load-bearing equation have a complete exposition packet?
- Does the simplest case reveal the general result rather than merely postpone it?
- Do results paragraphs report magnitudes and explain their source?
- Are literature, robustness, and extensions downstream of the claim they contextualize?
- Is repetition reinforcing one spine, or relaunching the paper?
- Did reordering preserve every assumption, estimate, citation, and qualification?

## Provenance

This protocol distills a close reading of Fagereng, Gomez, Gouin-Bonenfant, Holm, Moll, and Natvik,
[*Asset-Price Redistribution*](https://www.matthieugomez.com/files/apr.pdf), together with the
skill's existing reader-first and reasoning-graph guidance. It records transferable exposition
choices, not a claim that the paper supplies universal writing rules. Do not copy its language or
treat its organization as mandatory.
