# Anti-AI-Slop and Academic Prose Standard

## Contents

- Definition and governing tests
- Lexical and structural symptoms
- Academic and field-specific precision
- Editing protocol and final scan

## Definition

“AI slop” is not simply prose written by an AI. It is prose that sounds fluent while doing little intellectual work. It often uses generic framing, inflated abstraction, repetitive signposting, empty evaluation, excessive symmetry, or vague transitions in place of a precise claim and a named logical relation.

The goal is not to make prose austere or mechanical. The goal is to make every sentence earn its place.

## Governing tests

For every sentence, ask:

1. What exact claim, definition, operation, interpretation, or transition does this sentence perform?
2. Could the sentence apply unchanged to hundreds of unrelated papers?
3. Does it name the object and relation, or merely gesture at them?
4. Does it repeat the previous sentence without adding a premise, implication, or qualification?
5. Can a shorter sentence preserve all substantive content?
6. Does the wording imply more certainty, generality, novelty, or robustness than the evidence supports?

Delete, rewrite, or merge sentences that fail these tests.

## Common lexical symptoms

These phrases are not automatically forbidden, but they require scrutiny because they often carry no content:

- “It is important to note that...”
- “It is worth noting that...”
- “In this context...”
- “In today’s increasingly complex...”
- “This highlights/underscores the importance of...”
- “provides valuable insights into...”
- “offers a nuanced understanding of...”
- “sheds light on...”
- “serves as a useful framework for...”
- “plays a crucial/pivotal/central role...”
- “a rich set of...”
- “a complex interplay between...”
- “a multifaceted approach...”
- “a robust and comprehensive framework...”
- “from a broader perspective...”
- “taken together...” when used repeatedly or without synthesis;
- “moreover,” “furthermore,” and “additionally” when the relation is not merely additive;
- “not only X but also Y” when ordinary coordination is clearer;
- “both X and Y” repeated mechanically;
- “This result is intuitive” without stating the intuition;
- “The implications are significant” without naming an implication and benchmark;
- “This can be seen as...” when the sentence should state the relation directly.

Replace the phrase with the substantive relation. For example:

Weak:

> This result highlights the importance of network effects.

Stronger:

> Because the resolvent loads shocks from every tract, an own-tract regression omits responses transmitted through commuting and shopping links.

## Structural symptoms

### Generic throat-clearing

A paragraph begins with field-wide background that the intended reader already knows and that does not set up the exact problem.

Repair: begin with the unresolved question or tension.

### Repeated roadmap prose

The manuscript repeatedly says what it will do rather than doing it. Roadmaps appear at the introduction, section opening, subsection opening, and paragraph opening with little new information.

Repair: keep roadmaps only where they clarify a dependency or a non-obvious order.

### Summary loops

A paragraph restates the result of the preceding paragraph using different adjectives.

Repair: either add a new implication or delete the paragraph.

### Formulaic three-part lists

Repeated triads create a polished cadence but may conceal weak hierarchy.

Repair: use the number of parts the argument actually has. Explain hierarchy rather than seeking symmetry.

### Overbalanced prose

Every sentence uses the same pattern: “X does A, while Y does B.” Repeated parallelism sounds generated and can flatten differences in importance.

Repair: vary structure according to logic. Use parallelism only for genuine comparison.

### Canned paragraph endings

Paragraphs end with “These findings have important implications for...” without stating the implication.

Repair: state the exact next inference.

### Abstract noun stacks

Examples:

- “the spatial incidence transmission mechanism structure”;
- “the policy-relevant equilibrium adjustment channel.”

Repair: restore agents and verbs.

### Nominalization overload

Weak:

> The implementation of the estimation of the response requires the construction of exposure measures.

Stronger:

> Estimating the response requires constructed exposure measures.

### Hedge stacking

Weak:

> The result may potentially suggest that tourism could possibly lower welfare.

Stronger:

> Under the maintained mapping, the estimate implies lower nonhousing real income.

Use one calibrated hedge when uncertainty is real.

### Inflated claims

Words requiring evidence or a stated comparison include:

- novel;
- first;
- general;
- exact;
- structural;
- causal;
- robust;
- comprehensive;
- sharp;
- closed form;
- sufficient;
- universal;
- tractable;
- substantial;
- economically meaningful.

Do not delete these words automatically. Verify their conditions and make the comparison explicit.

### Negative-definition overload

A result is repeatedly explained through defensive statements:

> This is not X. It is not Y. It should not be read as Z.

Qualifications may be necessary, but repeated negative framing obscures the positive object.

Repair:

1. state what the object is;
2. state its use;
3. give the one or two scope conditions that matter.

Example:

Weak:

> This is not a structural income estimate and should not be interpreted as a direct pass-through coefficient.

Stronger:

> The origin fitted value is the component of bilateral spending associated with the constructed network exposures. It equals a structural nominal-income response only under the response-span condition.

### Vague demonstratives

Weak:

> This matters for the result.

Stronger:

> The zero-th-round normalization matters because it determines whether the direct and full-response coefficients can be converted into direct and tail loadings.

### Unmotivated “we” sentences

A sequence of “We now... We then... We next...” reports author actions but not logical necessity.

Repair: put the object or unresolved task in subject position.

Weak:

> We next introduce a nonlinear model.

Stronger:

> The local incidence estimates do not determine how tax bases adjust under large policy changes. A nonlinear model supplies that curvature.

## Academic prose principles

### Precision before elegance

Prefer the exact object to a polished near-synonym. If “fitted origin response” is the defined object, do not casually call it “income,” “budget gain,” “purchasing power,” and “origin effect” unless each transformation is stated.

### Old-to-new order

Begin with an active object and end with the new information that the next sentence will use.

### Put the relation in the verb

Prefer:

- “premultiplying constructs”;
- “market clearing maps”;
- “the projection recovers”;
- “the assumption eliminates”;
- “the special case sets”;
- “the coefficient loads”;
- “the appendix derives.”

Avoid vague verbs such as “relates to,” “speaks to,” “connects with,” or “captures” when a more exact operation is available.

### Keep subjects and verbs close

Reduce long introductory clauses and nested qualifications that separate the subject from the main verb.

### Consolidate qualifications

Define local terminology such as:

> Throughout this subsection, “implied price” means the CES mapping of the fitted destination response; structural equality additionally requires the response-span condition.

Then use the term consistently. Do not repeat the entire caveat after every claim.

### Use active voice selectively

Use active voice when it clarifies agency or operation:

> Premultiplying by \(S'\) forms the origin price index.

Use passive voice when the actor is irrelevant:

> Standard errors are clustered by origin-month and destination-month.

Do not mechanically convert every passive sentence.

### Control sentence rhythm

Avoid both extremes:

- a string of long, multi-clause sentences;
- a string of short declarative fragments.

Vary length in response to logical structure. Use a short sentence for a genuine conclusion or contrast, not as artificial drama.

### Use emphasis sparingly

Do not create many one-sentence paragraphs, italics, or “The key point is...” statements. Emphasis works only when the hierarchy is otherwise clear.

## Field-specific precision checks

In technical economics and quantitative social science, scan for silent shifts among:

- sales, expenditure, revenue, and income;
- budget, nominal income, and real income;
- producer price, delivered price, generalized price, and price index;
- direct shock, zero-th network round, full exposure, and tail;
- coefficient, fitted value, elasticity, loading, and derivative;
- residual shock and realized spending;
- causal identification and structural interpretation;
- local incidence and counterfactual welfare;
- retention and ownership;
- observed network topology and calibrated factor accounts.

A change in term should correspond to a stated mathematical or empirical transformation.

## Editing protocol

For every proposed rewrite:

1. preserve the technical claim;
2. identify the sentence’s job;
3. remove generic framing;
4. name the object;
5. name the logical relation;
6. preserve conditions;
7. remove repetition;
8. read the sentence with its neighbors;
9. ensure the rewrite does not introduce a stronger claim;
10. check that the report’s own explanation is not slop.

## Final anti-slop scan

Search the revised manuscript and review report for:

- repeated stock phrases;
- repeated “This section...” or “We now...” openings;
- unsupported evaluative adjectives;
- vague demonstratives;
- ambiguous “it” or “they”;
- conclusion words without premises;
- unnecessary adverbs such as “clearly,” “obviously,” “notably,” “importantly,” and “interestingly”;
- unnecessary “in order to”;
- hedge stacks;
- repeated negative qualifications;
- repeated three-part lists;
- terminology drift;
- paragraphs that summarize without advancing;
- generic final sentences.

Do not report a count alone. Review each occurrence in context.
