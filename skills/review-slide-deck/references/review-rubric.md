# Slide-deck review rubric

Use this rubric as a diagnostic framework. Do not force every presenter into the same visual style, and do not convert heuristics into arbitrary pass/fail rules.

## 1. Audience goal and opening

Determine what a realistic audience should understand, remember, or believe at the end. Check whether the opening quickly establishes:

- the economic or substantive question;
- why the question matters;
- the main answer or thesis;
- what the talk will show to support it.

Flag openings dominated by apologies, biography, literature chronology, excessive institutional detail, or a long mystery build. The audience should know the destination early.

## 2. Argument architecture

Reconstruct the causal and evidentiary chain. The deck should normally move from main point to main steps to supporting detail. Check whether:

- every section advances the central claim;
- transitions say why the next section is needed;
- brief recalls reconnect detail to the main point;
- terminology and notation remain stable;
- the conclusion answers the opening question rather than merely listing sections;
- later details can be cut without making earlier claims unintelligible.

Stable language is more valuable than stylistic variation when concepts recur. Established field notation is usually preferable unless a change creates a clear gain.

## 3. One-message discipline

For each slide, state its intended takeaway in one sentence. Flag a slide when:

- it contains multiple independent claims;
- its title is only a topic label and does not help the audience infer the point;
- important content appears without being discussed;
- the audience must read while also following a different spoken argument;
- an essential assumption or comparison is visually buried.

Prefer claim-style titles when they remain accurate. Split slides with incompatible jobs; move nonessential detail to backup. Do not shrink type to preserve an overloaded slide.

## 4. Prose, notation, and equations

Prefer short, direct sentences and active verbs. Check parallel structure in lists. Remove inflated transitions, redundant qualifiers, and phrases that delay the noun or verb.

For equations:

- state what question the equation answers before displaying it;
- define new symbols locally or make their meaning recoverable;
- visually emphasize the term or comparison being discussed;
- avoid presenting algebra that is never used in the spoken argument;
- separate a derivation from its economic interpretation;
- preserve consistent indices, units, signs, and normalization.

A mathematically correct slide can still fail if the audience cannot tell where to look or why the expression matters.

## 5. Figures and tables

Every figure or table should support one principal message. Check:

- an informative title or nearby claim;
- labeled axes, units, samples, and series;
- readable legends and annotations;
- sufficient contrast and distinguishability without relying only on color;
- visible source or provenance when needed;
- no unexplained denominator, normalization, or scale change;
- no more information than the speaker can discuss.

For tables, emphasize the estimates or comparisons being discussed and suppress nonessential digits and columns. For figures, reduce overlapping series and decorative elements. Counts such as “three series” or “ten numbers” are warning heuristics, not universal limits.

## 6. Visual accessibility and rendered quality

Inspect the actual rendered slides at normal presentation scale and, where relevant, on a laptop-sized viewport. Check:

- readable body text, labels, equations, footnotes, and citations;
- adequate contrast and color-blind-safe distinctions;
- no clipping, overflow, broken glyphs, missing images, or bad line wraps;
- consistent alignment, margins, and visual hierarchy;
- sufficient whitespace;
- descriptive link text and captions where the delivery format requires them;
- animations and builds reveal information in a meaningful order and do not hide evidence in a static export.

Hybrid and recorded talks need especially legible figures and captions because viewers may use small screens.

## 7. Timing and cutability

Estimate time slide by slide using content complexity. Sparse substantive slides may still require several minutes. Check whether the deck includes:

- time for the opening and conclusion;
- room for expected interruptions;
- explicit optional sections or skip points;
- a clear cut sequence;
- backup slides for derivations, robustness, data construction, and anticipated objections.

Flag a deck whose core argument consumes the entire nominal slot before questions. Going overtime is a substantive presentation failure.

## 8. Delivery and questions

Deck-only evidence cannot establish delivery quality, so label inferences. For a rehearsal-focused review, check:

- a scripted or stable opening sentence;
- direct transitions and periodic restatement of the main point;
- deliberate pauses after important claims and before answers;
- explanation directed to the audience rather than the screen;
- a plan for deferring questions that would derail the sequence;
- concise answers to clarifications and fuller answers to substantive objections;
- anticipated questions with supporting backup slides;
- a closing sentence that states the answer and implication.

The presenter should accept responsibility for clarity, maintain control of the sequence respectfully, and finish on time.

## 9. Integrity checks

Flag, but do not silently fix:

- deck claims that exceed the displayed evidence;
- changes in estimand, sample, unit, or baseline across slides;
- citations that are missing, unreadable, or not linked to the claim they support;
- paper-deck inconsistencies;
- animations or crops that visually exaggerate an effect;
- uncertainty, limitations, or equilibrium assumptions essential to interpreting the result but absent from the talk.

## Provenance

The audience-centered foundation of this rubric is a paraphrased adaptation of Berthold Herrendorf, *How to Make Academic Presentations* (September 11, 2021), <https://roozbeh-hosseini.github.io/PresentationTips.pdf>. In particular, it retains the source's emphasis on limited audience capacity, answer-first argument order, one message per slide, direct language, stable terminology, rehearsal, question preparation, and finishing on time. It extends the source with checks for accessibility, equations, citations, hybrid viewing, visual integrity, and source-versus-deck consistency.
